Ethereum Payments Integration: A Developer's Guide

How to integrate Ethereum payments: choose ETH or tokens, create payment requests, detect and confirm transactions, handle gas, secure keys and test.

Chapters
C
Chaingateway Team
Blockchain Experts

Integrating Ethereum payments means doing four things reliably: giving each customer a unique place to pay, detecting the transaction when it lands, deciding when it counts as final, and reconciling it against your order. You can build that yourself on top of nodes and JSON-RPC, or call a unified API that handles wallets, gas and notifications for you. This guide covers both paths, so you can pick one with your eyes open.

TL;DR

  • An Ethereum payment is a signed transaction that moves ETH or tokens. Submitting it is not the same as confirming it: you must check the receipt.
  • Decide first: ETH only, stablecoins and ERC-20 tokens, or your own token. That choice drives your event handling and your gas strategy.
  • Use one deposit address per order or user, and listen for events through webhooks instead of polling.
  • Treat private keys, webhook signatures and contract code as the three main security surfaces.
  • A unified API such as Chaingateway replaces the node, gas and decoding work, with plans from €49 per month.

What an Ethereum payment actually is

An Ethereum payment is a cryptographically signed instruction from an account that updates the state of the network, for example by transferring ETH or tokens. According to ethereum.org’s transaction documentation, a validator then checks the transaction and includes it in a block. Only after that does the money exist at the recipient’s address.

For your application this has a practical consequence. The customer does not pay you. They broadcast a transaction to the network, and your system has to notice it. Everything in an integration is a variation on that observation problem.

There are two kinds of payment you will meet:

  1. Native ETH transfers. The transaction itself carries a recipient and a value. Simple, and visible directly in the transaction and its receipt.
  2. Token transfers (ERC-20 and friends). The transaction goes to the token’s contract, which updates balances internally. The standard defines a Transfer event for this, as laid out in the ERC-20 specification on ethereum.org. Your system learns about the payment from that event log, not from the transaction’s own value field.

Most stablecoin checkouts are the second kind. If you only watch for ETH arriving at an address, you will miss them.

Decisions to make before you write code

Which assets will you accept?

Start with the narrowest set that serves your customers. ETH alone is the easiest to implement. Adding a stablecoin removes price volatility from the equation, which matters for invoices and subscriptions. If you issue your own token, you also need to know its decimals, its contract address and whether it behaves like a standard ERC-20.

Every extra asset adds a contract address to monitor, a decimals value to apply, and a pricing rule. Keep a small allowlist and reject anything else on purpose.

Custody and compliance

Who holds the keys? If your platform generates deposit wallets for customers, you are handling funds, and depending on your jurisdiction and business model that can carry licensing or reporting obligations. Ask a lawyer who knows your market before launch, not after. This guide is about the engineering, not legal advice.

The core payment flow, step by step

Here is the sequence that holds up in production, whether you build it or buy it.

1. Create a payment request

When the customer reaches checkout, create an order record with an amount, an asset and an expiry. Then assign a unique receiving address to that order. A unique address per order lets you match an incoming transfer to the right customer without asking them to paste a memo or reference.

Price the order in a stable unit, lock an ETH amount for a short window if you accept volatile assets, and store both numbers.

2. The customer signs and submits

If the customer pays from a browser wallet, your front end asks for permission to use their account, builds a transaction object and submits it. MetaMask’s eth_sendTransaction reference describes this order: get account permission, submit the transaction, keep the returned hash. If the customer instead sends from an exchange or a mobile wallet to your displayed address, you never see this step. You only see the result on-chain.

Either way, a transaction hash is the receipt number of the payment. Store it.

3. Detect the transaction

You have three options for noticing a payment:

  • Polling. Ask a node for the receipt or the address balance every few seconds. Simple, but wasteful, and fragile at scale.
  • Subscriptions. Open a WebSocket to a node and subscribe to new blocks or contract logs. The go-ethereum documentation on real-time events describes eth_subscribe for exactly this. You must handle dropped connections and replay missed blocks yourself.
  • Webhooks. A service watches the chain and calls your server when something relevant happens. This is the lowest-maintenance option and the one most teams choose.

For tokens, you are watching event logs. The Ethereum documentation notes that applications can listen for contract events through the JSON-RPC API and act on the logs they emit.

4. Confirm the result

Submission is not confirmation. The MetaMask send-transaction guide shows the pattern: poll for the receipt by hash; no receipt means the transaction is still pending, a status of 0x1 means success, and 0x0 means it failed. The eth_getTransactionReceipt reference adds that the receipt carries the block number, gas used and logs.

Your order state machine should reflect this explicitly:

  1. awaiting_payment: address issued, nothing seen.
  2. pending: a matching transaction is visible but not yet in a block.
  3. confirmed: receipt found, status successful, amount and asset match.
  4. final: enough additional blocks have passed for your risk tolerance.
  5. failed, underpaid, overpaid or expired: the cases that need a rule, not a shrug.

How many blocks to wait before releasing goods is a business decision. Digital goods under a few dollars can ship on the first confirmation. High-value orders deserve a longer wait, because a block can occasionally be reorganized before the network settles on it.

5. Reconcile

Compare, every day, the sum of confirmed payments in your database against what the chain says arrived at your addresses. Any difference is a bug, a missed webhook or a customer who paid the wrong asset. Data tooling can help here: the data and analytics section of ethereum.org lists indexers that serve historical event logs and queryable real-time data, which can reduce how much of this you maintain yourself.

Gas, failed transactions and refunds

Gas is where many first integrations get surprised. Every transaction pays a fee made of a base fee and a priority fee, and the sender pays it, as the transactions documentation explains. Three consequences follow for you.

Customers can underpay or stall. If they set a fee too low, the transaction sits pending. Your order should not expire while a valid transaction is in flight.

Failed transactions still cost gas. A reverted token transfer produces a receipt with a failed status, and the customer’s funds do not move. Do not mark the order paid because you saw a hash.

Sending from your side costs you. Refunds, sweeps from deposit addresses to a treasury wallet and payouts all require gas, and token addresses need ETH to move tokens. Estimate the fee before sending, and budget for it. Our guide on how to estimate gas fees on Ethereum and other chains walks through the estimation methods.

If you want customers to pay without holding ETH at all, account abstraction lets developers sponsor transactions, as described in the ethereum.org tutorial on sponsoring gas fees. It adds contract complexity, so reserve it for products where the gas friction really costs you conversions.

For refunds, decide the policy up front. Refund to the sending address only after checking that it is not an exchange deposit address you cannot control, or ask the customer for a refund address. Subtract the network fee openly, or absorb it, but write the rule down.

Securing keys, signatures and contracts

Private keys

Whoever holds the key controls the funds. Generate keys inside a hardened environment, never log them, never commit them, and separate hot wallets (small balances, used for payouts) from cold storage (most of the funds). Sweep deposit addresses to the cold side on a schedule.

Webhook authenticity

A webhook endpoint is a public URL that can mark orders as paid. Anyone who finds it can post a fake event. Verify a signature on every request (HMAC signing is the common approach), reject stale timestamps, and make the handler idempotent so a duplicate delivery does not credit an order twice. Then, before releasing goods, optionally confirm the transaction with a direct lookup.

Smart contract code

If you write your own payment contracts, the risk moves to the code. Ethereum’s own security report notes that audits, tested libraries, formal verification and static analysis all improve contract security, and that custom code for advanced controls can increase vulnerability risk. The practical rule: do not deploy a custom contract when a plain address and standard token events are enough for your use case.

For a deeper look at application-level review, a guide on what a web application audit service covers is a good checklist of what to ask for.

Build it yourself or use an API?

The do-it-yourself path means running or renting nodes, writing an address generator, building the log decoder for every token you accept, estimating gas, handling reorgs, and keeping all of it running. It is a reasonable path for a team whose product is the payment infrastructure.

For everyone else, the cost is time and ongoing maintenance. The architecture question is the same one companies face when connecting to partners generally: push events in real time rather than batch-reconcile later, a trade-off covered well in this piece on APIs for real-time B2B data. Payments are the same logic at a smaller scale.

If you want a map of the options before committing, our comparison of blockchain API types and when to use each lays out the categories, and the Ethereum overview explains ETH, gas and ERC-20 tokens for teams new to the chain.

Testing and monitoring before and after launch

A payment integration is not done when the demo works. Run through this list on a test network first, then again with small real amounts:

  • Exact payment, underpayment and overpayment of both ETH and each accepted token.
  • Late payment after the order expired, and duplicate payment to the same address.
  • Failed transaction with a low gas limit, so you see a failed receipt and confirm the order does not complete.
  • Duplicate and out-of-order webhooks, replayed deliberately.
  • Your own downtime: stop your server for ten minutes, send a payment, restart, and verify that nothing is lost.
  • Wallet flows in the front end. QA teams can regression-test them without holding private keys using tools such as the one listed on ethereum.org’s Impersonator.xyz page.

After launch, alert on three signals: orders stuck in pending longer than a threshold, a gap between database totals and on-chain totals, and webhook delivery failures. The first catches fee problems, the second catches missed events, the third catches your own outages.
