Polygon Payments Integration: A Developer's Guide

How to integrate payments on Polygon: choose the network and token, manage wallets, send and verify transfers, handle webhooks, and test before launch.

Chapters
C
Chaingateway Team
Blockchain Experts

A Polygon payments integration comes down to four jobs: create wallets, send token transfers, confirm them on-chain, and get notified when money arrives. You can build each piece against raw nodes, or call one REST API that handles wallets, transfers and webhooks for you. This guide covers both paths, so you can decide what to build and what to hand off.

TL;DR

  • Polygon PoS uses chain ID 137 and POL for gas. Stablecoin transfers there are cheap enough for checkout-sized payments.
  • A payment is only done when you have checked the transaction status, recipient, amount and token contract. A hash alone is not proof.
  • Use webhooks, not polling, to track payments. Verify the signature on the raw request body and make your handler idempotent.
  • Plan for legal scope, key custody, nonce conflicts and gas swings before launch, not after.
  • A unified API like Chaingateway’s Polygon API removes node operation and event decoding from the build.

What a Polygon payment integration actually includes

A payment integration is more than “send tokens.” A working gateway has to manage user wallets, track each transaction’s state through webhooks, and reconcile balances in a database, as one payment gateway architecture guide lays out. The chain only tells you what happened. Your backend decides what it means for an order, an invoice or a balance.

In practice, you build five components:

  1. Wallet layer: addresses to receive funds and keys to send them.
  2. Transfer layer: code that builds, signs and broadcasts token transfers.
  3. Verification layer: checks that a transfer really happened as expected.
  4. Notification layer: webhooks that tell your app about status changes.
  5. Ledger: your own database record of what is owed, paid and settled.

The first four are chain plumbing. The fifth is your product. The less time you spend on the plumbing, the sooner you ship the part customers see.

Settle the legal question first

Before writing code, work out where you operate and which licenses apply. The same architecture guide points to Money Transmitter Licenses in the US and VASP registration in the EU under MiCA as examples of what you may need to identify. Whether your model triggers any of them depends on whether you hold or move funds for others. That is a question for a lawyer who knows payments, not for a code review. Ask it before launch, because the answer can change whether you custody funds at all.

Choose the network and token

For most payment products, the decision is Polygon PoS with a stablecoin such as USDC or USDT. Polygon PoS uses chain ID 137 and POL for fees, and transfer costs move with network congestion and the POL price, according to Polygonscan’s gas fee explainer. Hard-code the chain ID in your signing code so a transaction can never be broadcast to the wrong network.

What fees to expect

Fees are the reason teams pick Polygon. Messari’s Q3 2025 report puts the average transaction fee that quarter at 0.0114 POL, about $0.0027. That is a snapshot, not a promise, so design your fee handling around ranges rather than one number. Blockworks describes Polygon as a high-throughput, payments-focused environment capable of supporting up to 5,000 payments per second.

The same Blockworks comparison notes that Ethereum remains a foundational choice for users who put security, liquidity and institutional-grade settlement first. That is a reasonable split of roles. Many products accept payments on Polygon for low cost and offer Ethereum for larger or more conservative customers. If you need both, one API across chains means you write the payment flow once. See the Ethereum API for the other side of that setup.

Funding the gas wallet

Your sending wallets need POL for gas even when the thing being sent is a stablecoin. A common, low-friction way to get balance onto Polygon is to pick Polygon as the withdrawal network at a trading venue instead of using a bridge, as CryptoTicker notes. Check the network label carefully before you confirm. A withdrawal on the wrong network is the most common way to lose funds during setup.

Set up wallets and protect the keys

Wallets are where most integrations get risky. There are two common patterns.

One address per customer or invoice. Each payment gets its own deposit address. Matching an incoming transfer to an order is then trivial, because the recipient address identifies it. The cost is more addresses to manage and, later, sweeping funds to a treasury wallet.

One shared address with references. Simpler to operate, but a plain token transfer carries no reference field. You end up matching on amount and timing, which breaks when two customers pay the same amount.

For most commerce flows, per-order addresses are the cleaner design. Whatever you choose, keep these habits:

  • Never keep private keys in source control, logs, or client-side code.
  • Separate receiving wallets from the treasury that holds your balance, so a compromise of one does not expose the other.
  • Limit who and what can trigger an outgoing transfer, and log every request.
  • Back up key material in a way that survives the loss of one person or one server.

If you would rather not build key handling yourself, an API can create wallets on request. Our walkthrough on creating a crypto wallet with an API shows the call flow and what to store on your side.

Send a token payment on Polygon

Polygon PoS is EVM-compatible, so token transfers follow the ERC-20 standard. The ERC-20 specification defines two payment patterns:

  • Direct payment: the payer calls transfer(recipient, amount), which emits a Transfer event.
  • Contract-mediated pull payment: the payer first calls approve(spender, amount), then the authorized contract calls transferFrom(sender, recipient, amount).

For a simple checkout, direct transfer is enough. Approvals matter when a smart contract collects money, such as subscriptions or escrow. Keep approvals narrow: approve the amount you need, not an unlimited allowance.

Doing it with raw node code

Sending directly means you handle everything: build the transaction, estimate gas, set the nonce, sign, broadcast, and watch for inclusion. That is manageable for a prototype. In production, you also need a reliable RPC provider, retry logic, and a way to replace stuck transactions. Our guide on sending ERC-20 tokens compares wallet, code and API methods side by side.

Doing it with one REST call

With a unified API, a transfer is a single authenticated request that names the chain, token, sender, recipient and amount. Gas estimation happens for you, and the request is HMAC-signed for security. The Polygon Blockchain API is built around USDC and USDT payments over REST, and the broader Blockchain API uses the same pattern for Bitcoin, Ethereum and other chains. Adding a second chain later becomes a parameter change rather than a new integration.

Verify that a payment really arrived

A transaction hash only proves a transaction was submitted. Before marking an order paid, check the transaction status, the recipient, the token transfer amount and the token contract. One Polygon transaction checking guide notes that ERC-20 transfers are recorded in Transfer event logs, so the log is where you read the real transfer.

The contract check is the one teams forget. Anyone can deploy a token named “USDC” that is worthless. Compare the contract address in the log with the address you whitelisted. Your verification routine should confirm all of these:

  1. The transaction succeeded, not just that it was mined.
  2. The Transfer event came from your expected token contract.
  3. The recipient matches the address you issued for this order.
  4. The amount is at least what you invoiced, in the token’s smallest unit.
  5. Enough blocks have passed for your risk tolerance before you release goods.

Reading raw logs means decoding topics and data by hand. Our explainer on the ERC-20 Transfer event shows the structure, and an API that returns decoded transaction data lets you skip that step entirely.

Get real-time notifications with webhooks

Polling an address every few seconds wastes requests and still lags. Webhooks flip it: the service calls your endpoint when something changes. If you want the mechanics, read how Polygon webhooks deliver on-chain events without polling.

Build the receiver defensively

Whatever provider you use, the same rules apply. One published description of Polygon’s own webhooks says they deliver status-change events to an HTTPS endpoint with an HMAC-SHA256 signature, and retry after a non-2xx response, timeout or network error, per the APIs.io AsyncAPI listing. The same page says signature verification must use the raw request body and a constant-time comparison. Both points shape how you write the handler:

  • Verify before parsing. Compute the HMAC over the raw bytes, not over re-serialized JSON, which can change whitespace or key order.
  • Compare in constant time. Use your language’s secure compare function, not ==.
  • Answer fast. Return a 2xx immediately and do the heavy work in a queue. A slow handler triggers retries.
  • Expect duplicates. Retries mean the same event can arrive more than once. Store an event or transaction ID and ignore repeats.
  • Expect out-of-order delivery. A “confirmed” event can arrive before “pending”. Move state forward only, never backward.

A state model that holds up

Keep payment states small and explicit: awaiting_payment, pending, confirmed, failed, expired. Each webhook maps to at most one transition. If a failed event arrives for something already confirmed, log it and alert a human instead of overwriting state. Chaingateway Webhooks delivers these notifications with HMAC-signed requests, so your receiver follows the pattern above.

Design the backend so it can grow

The cleanest payment backends keep chain-specific code at the edges. A modular, chain-agnostic architecture lets you add blockchain integrations without rebuilding core logic, a point the gateway architecture guide makes as well. Put an internal interface in front of the chain, with methods like createDepositAddress, sendPayment and getPaymentStatus, and keep your order logic on the other side of it.

If your business also takes card and bank payments, the same separation helps. Crypto becomes one more rail beside the others. For a view of how a merchant side handles crypto processing for merchants alongside conventional gateways, that is a good place to compare your payment flow against.

The ledger should be yours. Never treat the chain or a provider dashboard as your book of record. Store every expected payment, every observed transfer, and the link between them, so you can reconcile at day’s end and explain any gap.

Test and launch checklist

The monitoring side of a payment gateway should detect failed transactions, manage nonce conflicts and handle gas price fluctuations, as the architecture guide stresses. Build your tests around those three.

Before launch

  • Run the whole flow on a test network first: create an address, pay it, receive the webhook, mark the order paid.
  • Replay each webhook twice and confirm the second delivery changes nothing.
  • Send a payment in the wrong token, the wrong amount (under and over), and to an expired order. Decide and test what happens in each case.
  • Break your endpoint on purpose with a 500 error and a timeout, then confirm retries recover cleanly.
  • Fire two transfers from the same wallet at once to expose nonce conflicts.

At launch

  • Start with low limits and one token.
  • Alert on any transfer stuck pending beyond your threshold.
  • Watch the gas wallet balance, since an empty POL balance stops every outgoing payment.
  • Rotate webhook secrets on a schedule and keep the old one valid briefly during the swap.

Common mistakes

  • Trusting the hash instead of the event log.
  • Parsing the webhook body before verifying the signature.
  • Hard-coding a gas price.
  • Skipping the token contract check.
  • Keeping hot-wallet keys and treasury keys together.
