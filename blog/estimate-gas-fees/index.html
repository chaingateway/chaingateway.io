How to Estimate Gas Fees on Ethereum and Other Chains

Learn how to estimate gas fees: the formula, gas limit vs gas price, eth_estimateGas, and the traps on Arbitrum and other chains, with a worked example.

Chapters
C
Chaingateway Team
Blockchain Experts

To estimate a gas fee, multiply the gas a transaction will use by the price you pay per unit of gas. On Ethereum after EIP-1559, that means (base fee + priority fee) × gas units used. The units come from simulating your exact transaction. The price comes from the live network. This guide shows both halves, then covers what changes on layer 2s, with smart accounts, and when you build payments into an app.

(If you landed here looking for the price of fuel for your car, this page is about blockchain transaction fees.)

TL;DR

  • Fee = gas used × price per gas. Gas used measures computational work. The price per gas reflects demand.
  • Simulate the exact transaction with eth_estimateGas for the units, and read live fee data for the price. Refresh both right before signing.
  • A gas limit is a ceiling, not a charge. Unused gas is not consumed, but a failed transaction still pays for the work it did.
  • Rollups like Arbitrum add a layer 1 data cost. Use each chain’s own estimator, not an Ethereum mainnet assumption.
  • For payment apps, store the estimate, fee fields, timestamp and final receipt fee so you can reconcile later.

The formula behind every gas estimate

Ethereum.org defines gas as the unit that measures the computational effort needed to run operations on the network. Every transaction consumes some amount of it. You then pay a price for each unit. That gives two separate numbers:

  1. Gas units depend on what the transaction does. Sending ETH to a plain address is simple. Calling a smart contract is not.
  2. Price per gas depends on how busy the network is right now. It is quoted in gwei.

The point most calculators hide: changing your contract logic moves the first number, while congestion moves the second. A good interface shows both instead of one unexplained total.

Gwei and ETH

One gwei equals 0.000000001 ETH (10^-9). So to convert, multiply gas units by the price in gwei, then divide by 1,000,000,000 to get ETH. Multiply by the ETH price only if you want a dollar figure. Our gwei converter handles wei, gwei and ETH in both directions.

What EIP-1559 changed

Since EIP-1559, each block has a base fee per gas that moves up or down with demand, and the base fee is burned. On top of that, a transaction carries a priority fee (the tip) and a max fee, the most you are willing to pay per gas in total. You pay the base fee of the block that includes you plus your tip, as long as the sum stays under your max fee.

So a wallet quote has three moving parts: units, base fee and tip. The max fee is a safety cap, not a prediction.

Gas limit vs gas price

These two terms trip up most newcomers, so here they are side by side.

Gas limitGas price
What it isThe most gas units you allow the transaction to useWhat you pay per unit, in gwei
Set byYour estimate of the work involvedMarket demand, plus your tip
Raising itDoes not raise the fee by itselfRaises the fee directly
Too lowTransaction failsTransaction waits or is not included

The key behavior: a gas limit is a ceiling. A standard ETH transfer uses 21,000 gas, and unused allocated gas is returned. MetaMask notes that raising a limit manually does not necessarily raise the final fee because unused gas is not consumed. It also does not buy faster confirmation. A manual override only protects you from underestimation.

The danger runs the other way. If a contract runs out of gas during execution, it reverts but still consumes the gas you supplied. And computation is paid for even when a transaction reverts. That is why you simulate before you broadcast.

A worked example

The numbers below are illustrative, not live prices. Assume a base fee of 20 gwei and a priority fee of 2 gwei, so 22 gwei per gas.

TransactionGas unitsPrice per gasFee in gweiFee in ETH
Plain ETH transfer21,00022 gwei462,0000.000462
ERC-20 approval (typical wallet display)45,00022 gwei990,0000.00099

The 21,000 figure for a simple transfer comes from Ethereum.org. The 45,000 figure is what MetaMask commonly shows for an ERC-20 approval, but the real number depends on the contract and its state. A token transfer or a swap will differ again. That is the whole reason you cannot hardcode units for anything beyond a plain transfer.

To get a fiat figure, take 0.000462 ETH and multiply by the current ETH price. Do that last, and label it as approximate.

Estimating gas in code

If you are building rather than clicking, the process has four steps.

  1. Describe the full transaction intent. eth_estimateGas needs the chain, the destination address, the calldata and the value, and optionally fee fields. Change the token amount, the recipient or the contract state and the estimate can change. Never reuse the estimate from a different transfer.
  2. Call eth_estimateGas against a node. It returns the units required, usually as a hexadecimal number you must decode.
  3. Fetch current price recommendations. Ask a fee oracle or your node for a base fee and a tip suggestion at the speed you want (slow, standard, fast).
  4. Compute and present a range. Multiply units by the price, and show users a maximum or range. A quote is not a guarantee, and the network can move between estimation and inclusion.

Then add one habit that saves headaches: refresh the price immediately before signing. Gas price is demand-sensitive, so the same transaction can cost different amounts at different times. Ethereum.org points to block explorers and gas trackers for live data when you only need a quick check by hand.

Pad the limit, not the price

A small buffer on the gas limit is cheap insurance, since unused gas is not charged. Padding the price per gas, on the other hand, directly increases what you pay. Keep those two decisions separate in your code.

Layer 2 and other chains do not follow the mainnet formula

A single generic EVM formula can mislead. Rollups charge for two things: execution on the layer 2, and posting your transaction data to layer 1.

Arbitrum is a good example. Its documentation says the quick path is calling eth_estimateGas and multiplying by the child-chain gas price. The returned gas limit includes layer 2 computation plus a buffer for the layer 1 cost. That buffer depends on compressed calldata size and on parent-chain pricing, so the estimate can shift when layer 1 gets busy. For component-level accounting, the NodeInterface.gasEstimateComponents call splits out the total, the layer 1 buffer and base fee data. Arbitrum itself warns that estimates are approximate and actual fees may differ.

For a deeper walk-through, see our post on tracking live Arbitrum gas fees with NodeInterface and Nitro’s formula.

Tron works on an entirely different model. It charges in bandwidth and energy rather than a gas price, so none of the above applies directly. Our guide to TRON fees and the TRON fee calculator for USDT-TRC20 transfers cover that model.

The rule for multi-chain products: use each network’s own estimator, and keep chain-specific logic behind one interface.

Smart accounts and sponsored gas

If you use account abstraction with a paymaster, a plain transaction quote is not enough. ERC-7677 explains that paymaster estimation depends on context, because the wallet and bundler know how the user operation will actually be submitted. It says wallets should use paymaster-provided gas values for paymaster-specific fields, including post-operation gas, since bundlers may underestimate those costs.

In practice: estimate through the same wallet, paymaster and bundler path you will use to broadcast. Do not reuse a number from an ordinary transaction.

Common estimation mistakes

  • Showing one number with no context. Users should see the units, the price, and a maximum.
  • Using the wallet’s maximum limit as the expected charge. The limit is a ceiling. Multiplying it by the price overstates the likely fee.
  • Estimating once and signing later. Prices and contract state move. Re-simulate right before signing.
  • Applying Ethereum mainnet assumptions to a rollup. You will miss the layer 1 data component.
  • Ignoring failures. A reverted transaction still costs gas. Simulate first and surface errors before the user pays.
  • Dropping the audit trail. Without records, finance cannot reconcile quotes against what was actually charged.

What to record for payments

If your app moves money, treat the estimate as data worth keeping. For each transaction, store:

  • the chain and block context
  • the gas estimate and the gas limit you set
  • the fee fields you used (base fee, tip, max fee)
  • the timestamp of the estimate
  • the final fee from the transaction receipt

Comparing the quote with the receipt tells you how far your estimates drift and whether your buffers are too tight or too generous. It also gives support a straight answer when a customer asks why a fee changed.
